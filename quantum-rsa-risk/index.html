Cryptography, explained

RSA is living on borrowed time

Two very different technologies are closing in on the encryption that carries most of the internet. Quantum computers promise the mathematics to break RSA outright. AI does not break the mathematics, but it is speeding up the hardware, finding the cracks in the implementation, and compressing the timetable. Neither has won yet — and yet the traffic you send today is already in the firing line.

The real question is not “can anyone break RSA today?” Nobody can. It is: two threats with different mechanics are arriving on different clocks, and one of them — harvest now, decrypt later — has already started. Quantum computing is the weapon. AI is the accelerator and the lockpick for everything around the lock.

Start with the distinction

Quantum and AI threaten RSA in completely different ways

Quantum computing

The existential, mathematical threat

In 1994 Peter Shor proved that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could factor large numbers exponentially faster than any known classical method. Factoring is the problem RSA is built on, so this is not a better lockpick — it is a key that fits every RSA lock.

+
Artificial intelligence

The accelerator and the opportunist

AI cannot guess a 2048-bit key. What it can do is calibrate qubits, tune error correction, and spot the subtle power and electromagnetic leaks that let an attacker extract a key from a running server. It shortens the timetable and attacks the implementation.

Confusing the two produces either panic (“AI will guess your key next year”) or complacency (“nothing matters until a quantum computer exists”). Both are wrong, for reasons the sections below separate out.

Mechanism

Why a quantum computer does not care how big the key is

Classical approach

Search, sequentially

The best known classical method — the general number field sieve — does not test key combinations one by one, but it is still exponential in the length of the number. Doubling the key size roughly squares the difficulty. Throwing more hardware at it only buys linear gains.

Academic records are the tell: RSA-512 (155 digits) fell in 1999, RSA-250 (829 bits) fell in 2020 after roughly 2,700 core-years of computation. RSA-2048 has never come close.

Shor's algorithm, 1994

Interference, in parallel

Shor recasts factoring as a question about the repeating pattern — the period — of a mathematical function. A quantum computer evaluates many inputs at once as overlapping waves, then arranges the interference so wrong answers cancel and the right one survives. Factoring becomes polynomial in the number's length.

The catch: interference is fragile. Holding those waves steady long enough to run the algorithm needs error correction and millions of physical qubits, not the noisy hundreds or low thousands that exist today.

A “cryptographically relevant quantum computer” (CRQC) is a specific thing: a fault-tolerant machine big enough to run Shor against real keys. Today's quantum computers are small, noisy and error-prone. Where the finish line sits is genuinely uncertain — the 20-million-qubit estimate has been revised downward repeatedly, and those revisions are the main reason the timeline has moved closer.

The other technology

What AI actually does to RSA — and what it cannot

Builds the weapon

AI accelerates quantum hardware

Stable qubits are a control-engineering nightmare. Machine learning models calibrate qubits, optimise control pulses, tune gate fidelities, decode error-correction codes in real time and hunt for better materials. In short, AI is helping engineers reach the CRQC finish line sooner.

Attacks the implementation

AI excels at side-channel attacks

A physical server leaks information while it works: micro-fluctuations in power draw, electromagnetic radiation, timing differences. Deep-learning models are very good at pulling a key out of noisy traces that defeated older statistical methods — and at finding flaws automatically in the software around the cryptography.

Does not break the maths

AI cannot guess a 2048-bit key

Exhausting an RSA-2048 keyspace means trying roughly 22048 possibilities — a number that dwarfs the atoms in the observable universe. No known machine-learning technique turns that exponential problem into a small one. AI finds weaknesses; it does not repeal arithmetic.

One asymmetry is worth holding on to: AI's gains against RSA are mostly gains against implementations — the code, the chip, the power supply. Shor's gains are against the mathematics itself. Defences therefore differ: better engineering and shielding beat the first; only post-quantum keys beat the second.

The response

The upgrade is already underway

Standards exist

In August 2024 NIST published the first post-quantum standards: ML-KEM (key exchange), ML-DSA and SLH-DSA (digital signatures), all built on problems believed hard for both classical and quantum computers. NIST's draft transition guidance marks RSA-2048 as deprecated by 2030 and disallowed by 2035.

Deployment is live rather than theoretical: browsers, cloud providers and messaging apps already negotiate hybrid handshakes that combine a classical algorithm with a post-quantum one, so a future break of either half alone does not expose the session.

What migration looks like

  1. Inventory. Find where RSA and other public-key algorithms are used — TLS, VPNs, code signing, certificates, firmware updates.
  2. Prioritise by data lifetime. Anything needing confidentiality for a decade is exposed to harvest-now-decrypt-later collection today.
  3. Deploy hybrid now. Layer ML-KEM alongside existing key exchange rather than switching atomically.
  4. Stay crypto-agile. Assume post-quantum algorithms will also be revised; make swapping algorithms a configuration change, not a rewrite.

Symmetric cryptography is a different story: Grover's algorithm only halves the effective strength of AES, so AES-256 remains comfortable. The urgent work is concentrated on public-key systems — RSA, Diffie-Hellman and elliptic curves.

Bottom line

Is the sky falling?

What is real

Shor's algorithm is proven mathematics, not speculation. Estimates of the resources needed to break RSA-2048 have fallen, not risen. Harvest-now-decrypt-later collection is something nation-states are widely assessed to be doing. Migration deadlines from NIST and the UK's NCSC exist precisely because the risk is treated as active.

What is overhyped

That AI is about to guess RSA keys — it isn't, and there is no known path for it to. That quantum computers are ready now — today's machines are nowhere near running Shor against real keys. And that this is an all-or-nothing collapse: symmetric encryption survives, hybrid deployments already ship, and the standards needed to migrate are finished.

The cryptography is not broken yet. The clock, the stored traffic and the migration schedule already are.

Firm Foundations, explained

What makes electricity cheap?

Onward’s 18 August 2026 paper argues that Britain has focused on the cost of generating a megawatt-hour while undercounting the cost of delivering reliable power across time and place. Its alternative accepts more gas and more emissions in exchange for a smaller, more “firm” power system.

The report’s real question is not “are renewables cheap?” It is: what does the whole power system cost once reliability, networks and balancing are included—and how much extra carbon are we willing to accept to lower that cost?

Start with the distinction

Generation cost is not system cost

One generator

Levelised cost

The average lifetime cost of each unit a plant produces. Useful for comparing projects, but it does not by itself value when or where the electricity arrives.

+
Keeping the grid usable

Integration costs

Transmission, congestion, balancing, storage, ancillary services and enough dispatchable capacity for low-wind periods.

=
The service consumers need

System cost

Reliable electricity delivered when and where it is wanted. The report says policy should optimise this total—not the first box alone.

This is the report’s strongest conceptual point. But allocating every network or balancing pound to a particular technology is difficult: costs depend on the entire generation mix, geography, demand profile and market design.

How to read the result

Not all “savings” mean the same thing

Physical

Build and operate less infrastructure

Lower network, balancing and ancillary costs may represent real resource savings—if the assumed generation geography, capacity mix and slower peak-demand growth materialise.

Policy transfer

Remove carbon pricing

£94bn of the headline comes from setting the power sector’s carbon price to zero from 2031. Bills fall, but the emissions externality and foregone public revenue do not vanish.

Different outcome

Use less electricity

The alternative reaches 441 TWh in 2050 versus 484 TWh under BAU, with less policy-driven heating, transport and hydrogen demand. Some avoided network spending accompanies less electrification.

Execution bet

Build nuclear better

The alternative assumes 20 GW of nuclear by 2050 and depends on lower construction costs and faster delivery. The model itself says future nuclear costs remain uncertain.

The carbon bargain

Cheaper electricity, dirtier electricity

23%unabated gas share in APP generation, 2050

The alternative’s power-sector intensity remains just below 100g CO₂/kWh in 2050, while BAU approaches zero and later becomes net-negative in the model because it includes bioenergy with carbon capture.

Onward argues that cheaper power makes heat pumps and electric vehicles attractive enough to cut emissions elsewhere. But Transira explicitly does not model economy-wide emissions, household bills or effects in heat, transport and industry. That wider “cheap power accelerates electrification” payoff is therefore an argument layered onto the power-system model, not an output of it.

Bottom line

A useful challenge, not a settled £320bn answer

What holds up

Levelised generation costs are not whole-system costs. Reliability and location matter. Network utilisation, connection delays and balancing deserve more weight. Exposing projects to more of the costs they cause is a serious reform agenda.

What the headline hides

The comparison changes several things at once: carbon pricing, demand, technology support, nuclear delivery, gas exposure and emissions. The £320bn is conditional on that whole bundle. It cannot by itself prove that renewables are intrinsically expensive or that the alternative is cheaper after valuing carbon and economy-wide outcomes.

The report is best read as a stress test of Britain’s current pathway—not as a forecast of a guaranteed saving.

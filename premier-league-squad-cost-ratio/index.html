Premier League finance · from 2026/27

The new cost of a squad

Squad Cost Ratio replaces the old profit-and-loss test with a seasonal question: how much of a club’s football income is being spent on the team?

85%

the green threshold

The shortcut: add player and head-coach wages, agent fees and annual transfer costs; divide by football-related revenue plus averaged player-trading results. At or below 85% is green. Above it brings scrutiny and, eventually, a levy. The starting red line is 115%—cross it and points are at stake.

Purpose and pressure

What is the rule trying to achieve—and who does it favour?

The League’s case is that clubs should be able to aspire and invest, but squad spending should remain connected to recurring football income. In-season checks are meant to expose problems earlier, while leaving stadiums, academies, women’s football and other off-pitch investment outside the cost cap.

More comfortable

Big, dependable revenues

Clubs with large commercial, broadcast, matchday and European income get a larger spending base. If their existing wage bill is controlled, they can add talent without approaching 85%.

More comfortable

Good player traders

Clubs that recruit or develop players cheaply and sell well can add net player-trading profit to the denominator. A productive academy therefore creates both players and regulatory headroom.

More freedom

Off-pitch builders

Investment in facilities, the academy, women’s football and commercial growth is not treated as squad cost. The route to a bigger future playing budget is to grow football revenue first.

More constrained

Ambitious, owner-backed challengers

A wealthy owner cannot simply turn fresh equity into SCR revenue. A club whose sporting ambition has grown faster than its turnover can have money available but too little ratio headroom to spend it on players.

More constrained

High wages and hard-to-sell squads

Long, expensive contracts keep the numerator high. If unwanted players cannot be sold, new signings add wages, agents’ fees and amortisation before old costs have left.

Two rulebooks

Clubs qualifying for Europe

They still face UEFA’s tighter 70% rule. Qualification raises revenue, but a fast-growing challenger can discover that European success also brings a lower permitted ratio.

Why player sales feel compulsory

Sell one player, create room for several annual costs

A sale helps twice: it removes the player’s future wages and accounting cost, and any qualifying net player-trading profit increases football resources. Academy players are especially attractive in accounting terms because they usually have little or no transfer book value, so most of the fee becomes profit.

A fair criticism—with an important distinction

The rule can make a willing owner unable to spend

Supporters have reasonably objected when ambitious clubs sell valued players despite having owners willing to fund further investment. Some prominent examples of that complaint arose under the old PSR loss calculation, not Premier League SCR. The two systems should not be treated as identical.

The underlying tension does carry over. Under SCR, owner funding does not itself enlarge the revenue base, while player-trading profit does. An ambitious club whose turnover has not caught up with its sporting plans can therefore be pushed towards sales even when it is neither short of cash nor trying to reduce its ambition.

SCR smooths player trading and tests squad cost seasonally, so it should reduce the incentive for one last-minute sale purely to repair a rolling three-year loss figure. But it does not remove the broader incentive to monetise players in order to create headroom.

The central trade-off

Linking spending to revenue reduces the chance that a club builds a wage bill it cannot sustain. But it can also protect the advantage of incumbents: historic success produces revenue, revenue permits spending, and spending helps preserve success. The 85% threshold and 30-point allowance give challengers more room than UEFA’s system, but they do not make owner wealth equivalent to club income.

    Actual revenues and costs replace estimates for clubs that were above green.

  4. OctoberFinal follow-up

    Further post-season assessment where required.

Transition: PSR governs 2025/26 while SCR runs in shadow. SCR is fully implemented in 2026/27; levies become payable for breaches from 2027/28. Historic PSR cases can still be pursued.

The mental model

SCR limits the team, not the whole club.

PSR asked whether a club lost too much money over three years. SCR asks, every season, whether football income can support football costs. It creates quicker signals and preserves freedom to build a stadium, academy or commercial operation—but an 85% squad ratio can still leave very little income to cover everything else.
